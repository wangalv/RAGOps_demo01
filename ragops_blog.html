From RAG PoC to Production: What the Demo Doesn't Tell You

A practical study of retrieval quality, Recall, Precision, Top-K, and the engineering gap between a successful proof of concept and a reliable production RAG system.

Abstract

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is increasingly used to connect large language models with enterprise knowledge. While a RAG proof of concept can often demonstrate impressive results quickly, the transition to production introduces a different set of questions around retrieval coverage, relevance, evaluation methodology, cost, latency, and ongoing quality management. This study examines that transition through a series of retrieval experiments conducted against the NSW Health Services Act 1997. The study progressed from a small PoC-oriented benchmark to a broader 79-question evaluation set and tested chunking, parent-child retrieval, query routing, HyDE, and LLM-based reranking. The results illustrate an important distinction: a strong PoC result demonstrates that a RAG approach can work under a defined set of conditions; it does not, by itself, establish that the system will remain reliable when exposed to broader and less predictable user queries. The study therefore frames RAG production readiness as an evaluation and engineering problem, rather than simply a model-selection or infrastructure problem.

1. Introduction: When a RAG PoC Looks Better Than Production

RAG demonstrations are often deceptively convincing. A document set is indexed, a user submits a question, the system retrieves several relevant passages, and an LLM produces a clear answer grounded in those passages. When this works consistently across a small set of examples, it is natural for a project team to conclude that the core problem has been solved and that the remaining work is primarily implementation and deployment.

However, the transition from a proof of concept to production changes the nature of the problem. Production users do not necessarily ask questions in the same terminology as the source documents, and they do not necessarily follow the structure assumed by the original test cases. Documents change, new query patterns emerge, retrieval strategies are modified, and the underlying models may be replaced. A system that performs well against a carefully constructed PoC dataset may therefore behave very differently under broader operating conditions.

This study explores that gap. Rather than treating RAG as a single end-to-end capability, it focuses first on the retrieval layer and asks a fundamental question: how reliably does the system place the information required to answer a question inside the LLM's context?

A successful RAG demo proves that RAG can work. It does not prove that RAG will work reliably in production.

2. RAG Fundamentals

Before discussing the experiments, it is useful to establish the basic mechanics of Retrieval-Augmented Generation. RAG combines an information retrieval process with a generative language model. Instead of asking an LLM to answer entirely from knowledge encoded in its parameters, the system first retrieves relevant information from an external knowledge source and then supplies that information as context for generation.

User Question
      ↓
Retrieve relevant document chunks
      ↓
Provide retrieved context to the LLM
      ↓
LLM generates the answer

In a typical enterprise scenario, the knowledge source may contain policies, procedures, legislation, technical documentation, contracts, product information, or other organisational content. The user asks a natural-language question; the retrieval layer searches the indexed knowledge; and the generation layer produces an answer based on the retrieved evidence.

This architecture creates two distinct quality questions. The first is whether the system found the right information. The second is whether the LLM used that information correctly. These questions correspond broadly to the retrieval and generation stages of the RAG pipeline, and they should not be treated as the same measurement problem.

3. Retrieval Quality: Recall, Precision and Top-K

3.1 Recall@K — Did We Find the Answer?

Recall is concerned with coverage. In a RAG system, Recall@K asks whether the relevant information appears within the top K retrieved results. For example, if a system retrieves five chunks for each question, Recall@5 measures how often the correct evidence is present somewhere in those five results.

100 questions
80 questions → correct passage found in top 5
20 questions → correct passage not found
Recall@5 = 80%

This should not be interpreted as saying that 80% of the final answers are correct. It means that for 80% of the questions, the retrieval system supplied the LLM with the relevant evidence needed to answer the question. For the remaining 20%, the correct evidence was not present in the retrieved set.

This distinction becomes critical in production. If the relevant passage is never retrieved, the generation model has no reliable opportunity to use it. Prompt engineering cannot recover information that was excluded from the context in the first place.

3.2 Precision@K — How Much Noise Did We Retrieve?

Precision addresses a different aspect of retrieval quality. Instead of asking whether the relevant information was found, Precision@K asks how much of the retrieved material is actually relevant.

Chunk 1 → Relevant
Chunk 2 → Irrelevant
Chunk 3 → Irrelevant
Chunk 4 → Relevant
Chunk 5 → Irrelevant

In this example, two of the five retrieved chunks are relevant.

Precision@5 = 40%

The system has found the answer, but it has also returned considerable noise. This matters because irrelevant context consumes tokens, can increase cost and latency, and may make it harder for the generation model to identify the evidence that actually matters.

Recall and precision therefore describe different dimensions of retrieval quality. Higher recall reduces the risk of missing the answer, while higher precision reduces the amount of irrelevant information introduced into the context. A production RAG system needs to understand both rather than optimising for one metric in isolation.

3.3 Top-K — How Much Should We Retrieve?

K represents the number of results returned by the retrieval system. If K is 5, the top five results are passed to the next stage; if K is 10, the system returns ten. Increasing K can improve the probability that a relevant passage is included, but it can also introduce more irrelevant context.

This is one reason why saying that "the RAG works" is not, by itself, an evaluation strategy. A meaningful evaluation needs to define what constitutes a relevant retrieval and measure how consistently the system achieves it.

4. Phase 1 — The PoC Baseline

The first phase of the study deliberately reflected the type of evaluation commonly used during a RAG proof of concept. A small, manually designed Golden Set of 21 questions was created against the NSW Health Services Act 1997. The questions were designed with knowledge of the underlying document structure and represented relatively well-defined information needs.

Recall@3 = 0.881

The initial result was strong: the correct passage appeared within the top three retrieved results for approximately 88% of the questions. In a typical PoC setting, a result of this kind can provide compelling evidence that the selected RAG architecture is viable.

However, the result answered a relatively narrow question: Can this retrieval pipeline find relevant information for this particular set of questions? It did not yet answer the more difficult production question: How will it behave when users express the same information need in ways that were not anticipated during the PoC?

The benchmark itself therefore became the next subject of investigation.

5. Phase 2 — Broadening the Benchmark

The original 21 questions had an important characteristic: they were created by someone who already understood the Act. They were clean, relatively predictable, and aligned with the structure of the source material. Real users are not constrained in the same way.

A user may ask, "What does Section 42 say?", but they may instead ask, "What happens if someone does this?", "Is there a penalty for doing X?", or "Does this requirement apply when the person is operating under condition Y?" The information need may be the same while the vocabulary and sentence structure are substantially different from the language used in the legislation.

To expose this issue, the benchmark was expanded to 79 questions, covering a broader range of realistic query patterns. The change in benchmark design produced a significant change in the measured baseline.

Recall@5 = 0.750

Precision@5 was approximately:

Precision@5 ≈ 0.169

The difference between the initial PoC result and the broader benchmark was one of the central observations of the study. The retrieval system itself had not necessarily become worse; rather, the evaluation set was now asking it to solve a more representative and more difficult problem.

A benchmark is not merely a measurement tool. It defines the problem against which "good" performance is being measured.

6. Phase 3 — Retrieval Engineering

Once the broader benchmark exposed weaknesses in the baseline, the next phase focused on retrieval engineering. Several structural approaches were evaluated to determine whether the retrieval process could improve without fundamentally changing the overall RAG architecture.

6.1 Better Chunking

Instead of treating large sections of legislation as single retrieval units, more granular subsection-level chunks were explored. The underlying hypothesis was that smaller and more semantically focused retrieval units might give the retriever more precise targets.

6.2 Parent-Child Retrieval

Parent-child retrieval was used to separate the unit used for retrieval from the context ultimately returned to the LLM. A smaller child unit can provide a precise retrieval target, while the associated parent context can preserve the broader information needed to interpret the result.

6.3 Query Routing

Different questions can represent different information needs. A question about a penalty may have a different retrieval pattern from a question about an obligation, prohibition, definition, or cross-reference. Query routing therefore explores whether an LLM can classify the question and direct it toward a retrieval strategy appropriate to that query type.

6.4 HyDE

HyDE, or Hypothetical Document Embeddings, takes a different approach to the vocabulary gap between user questions and source documents. Rather than embedding only the original question, an LLM first generates a hypothetical document-like response, which can then be used as part of the retrieval process. The underlying motivation is to make the representation used for retrieval more similar to the language and structure of the target documents.

7. The Surprising Result

After combining these structural techniques, the measured Recall@5 increased only marginally.

Recall@5 = 0.753

The baseline was:

Recall@5 = 0.750

The net change was therefore only +0.003.

This was an important finding because it demonstrated that architectural sophistication does not automatically translate into measurable retrieval improvement. Techniques such as improved chunking, parent-child retrieval, query routing, and HyDE can be useful in the right circumstances, but their value needs to be demonstrated against the actual evaluation set rather than assumed from their theoretical appeal.

In other words, production RAG engineering requires experimentation and measurement. A more complex pipeline is not necessarily a better pipeline.

8. Phase 4 — Reranking

The next experiment introduced an additional reranking stage. Instead of relying entirely on the initial similarity-based ranking, a larger candidate set was retrieved and an LLM was used to assess the relevance of the candidate passages. The results were then reordered according to the relevance assessment, and the highest-quality passages were retained for downstream generation.

This approach produced a more substantial improvement.

Recall@5 = 0.802

Compared with the Phase 3 result of 0.753, this represented an improvement of approximately 5.2 percentage points.

However, the improvement came with an operational trade-off. LLM-based reranking requires additional model inference for each query, which introduces additional latency and cost. The engineering question therefore changed from "Can retrieval quality be improved?" to a more production-oriented question:

Is the additional retrieval quality worth the additional latency and cost?

This is an example of the difference between optimisation in a PoC and optimisation in production. In a PoC, the objective may simply be to demonstrate technical feasibility. In production, quality must be considered together with cost, latency, scalability, maintainability, and operational risk.

9. What the PoC Did Not Reveal

The experiments changed the way the RAG pipeline could be viewed. A production RAG system is not simply:

Documents → Vector Database → LLM

Instead, it contains several quality surfaces that can fail independently.

9.1 Retrieval Quality

The retrieval layer needs to answer whether the right information was found and whether the retrieved context is sufficiently relevant. Useful measurements include Recall@K, Precision@K, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), nDCG, and retrieval latency.

9.2 Generation Quality

Even when the correct information is retrieved, the LLM can still produce an incorrect or unsupported response. Generation evaluation therefore introduces measures such as faithfulness, answer relevance, citation accuracy, hallucination rate, and context utilisation.

9.3 Operational Quality

Finally, the production system must operate in an environment where the knowledge base, models, retrieval strategies, and user behaviour evolve. Questions therefore emerge around document changes, amendments to legislation, new query types, embedding-model changes, chunking changes, regression detection, cost, latency, and post-deployment monitoring.

This third layer is where the concept of RAGOps becomes particularly relevant.

10. Production RAG Is a Moving Target

Consider a RAG system deployed today. Six months later, a source document may have changed, a new regulation may have been introduced, users may be asking different types of questions, the embedding model may have been replaced, and the team may have introduced query expansion or reranking. The LLM itself may also have changed.

At that point, the deployed system is no longer identical to the system evaluated during the original PoC. Yet without a repeatable evaluation process, the organisation may continue to reference the original benchmark result as evidence of system quality.

This is why RAG evaluation cannot reasonably be treated as a one-off activity. The evaluation process needs to become part of the engineering lifecycle so that changes to the system can be measured rather than assumed to be improvements.

11. From RAG Evaluation to RAGOps

The central implication of this study is that RAGOps should not be understood simply as application monitoring. It is better viewed as an engineering discipline for continuously evaluating the quality and behaviour of a RAG system as the system, knowledge base, models, and user queries evolve.

A production-oriented evaluation loop can be represented as follows:

Under this model, the benchmark becomes part of the product rather than a disposable artefact created for a PoC presentation. Retrieval and generation evaluations become repeatable tests that can be run when models, prompts, chunking strategies, or retrieval algorithms change.

The objective is not to claim that a RAG system has a single permanent accuracy number. The objective is to establish an evidence-based process for understanding how the system behaves, where it fails, and whether a change produces a measurable improvement.

12. Discussion

The progression across the four phases illustrates several broader observations about production RAG engineering.

First, benchmark design matters. A small, clean Golden Set can be useful for establishing whether the basic architecture works, but it may not adequately represent the linguistic diversity and ambiguity of real user queries. Expanding the evaluation set can reveal weaknesses that were hidden by the original benchmark.

Second, retrieval metrics need to be interpreted precisely. Recall, precision, and Top-K describe retrieval behaviour; they do not directly represent the correctness of the final generated answer. A high Recall@K can coexist with poor answer quality, while a strong final answer can sometimes be produced even when retrieval metrics are imperfect.

Third, engineering complexity should be justified by measurement. The Phase 3 experiments added several retrieval techniques but produced only a marginal change in Recall@5. By contrast, reranking produced a more noticeable improvement but introduced additional inference cost and latency. These results reinforce the need to evaluate architecture changes empirically.

Finally, production readiness is a continuous evaluation problem. Once the system is deployed, the environment changes. The benchmark, evaluation pipeline, and monitoring process therefore need to evolve with the system.

13. Conclusion

A RAG PoC typically answers a relatively simple question:

"Can we make this work?"

Production requires a broader set of questions:

"How often does it work, and under what conditions?"
"When does it fail, and can we detect those failures?"
"Can we reproduce a failure and determine what caused it?"
"Can we measure whether a change actually improved the system?"
"What happens when the knowledge base and user behaviour change?"

The gap between a RAG demo and a production RAG system is therefore not simply a matter of deploying the same architecture at larger scale. It is a shift from demonstrating capability to establishing measurable, repeatable, and continuously maintained evidence of quality.

This study began with a relatively narrow question — how good is the retrieval? — but the experiments led to a broader conclusion:

The difficult part of production RAG is not getting an LLM to answer questions from documents. It is building an engineering system that can continuously demonstrate that the answers are grounded in the right information.

That is the problem RAGOps is ultimately trying to address.
